The problem

An escalation costs you twice

Support cannot answer, so the ticket waits. An engineer picks it up, loses an afternoon to context they do not have, and finds a boring answer. Both people paid, and the customer waited for both.

How it works

Five steps, every time, in about a second

  1. Read the ticket

    Work out what it is about and who it is from. The account comes from the address it arrived on — never from the body, which anyone can write.

  2. Pick a playbook

    A playbook is an investigation you have described once: which systems to look at, in what order, and what each answer means.

  3. Query your live systems, read-only

    Through a database role you create and can inspect, scoped to that one customer, row-limited, timed out, and logged.

  4. Decide whose problem it is

    Ours, theirs, a third party's — or, when the evidence does not support an answer, that it does not know.

  5. Post the answer

    To your private review queue, with every query it ran attached. Your support lead marks it right or wrong. That is how the playbooks get better.

The part that matters

No generated SQL, ever

The obvious way to build this is to hand a language model your schema and let it write queries. It demos well. It is also a query nobody reviewed, against production data, on behalf of a ticket someone else wrote.

DNDD never does that. A playbook is structured data — a table, filters, columns, an order — which the engine assembles into a parameterised query. Table and column names are checked against your real schema when the service starts, so a playbook naming a column you do not have fails on startup rather than during someone's ticket.

Every read a playbook can perform is therefore knowable before it runs. That is what makes the list further down a set of facts rather than assurances. 

# a real step from a real playbook
- id: attempts
  shape: rows
  table: payment_attempts
  where:
    created_at: { gte: $window.start }
  select: [id, status, gateway_code, amount]
  order: [-created_at]
  limit: 20

# it names no account. this is what runs:
#   WHERE created_at >= :p1
#     AND account_id = :subject_id  ← added by us,

What it concludes

Three verdicts, and the honesty to give none

Every investigation ends in one of these, with the evidence that got it there.

NOT OUR BUG

Theirs

The issuer declined it. The request reached them and came back refused. Your support agent can say so, with the code and the timestamp.

OURS, ESCALATED

Ours

Something in your systems failed, and here are the error-tracker events from the same minute. This one should reach an engineer — and now it arrives with its evidence.

THIRD PARTY

Somebody else's

Your payment processor was having an incident at the time. Nobody needs to debug it; the answer is to retry.

And when the evidence does not support any of them, it says it does not know and asks for a human. That is a feature with a cost we chose on purpose: a system that always has an answer is a system that is sometimes confidently wrong, and one confidently wrong answer costs more trust than ten honest shrugs.

Security brief

Nothing leaves your network

The answer sheet, so your platform team can check it rather than take our word.

  • One Helm chart. It runs in your cluster, beside the systems it reads.
  • No inbound connections. We cannot reach it. Neither can anyone else.
  • Your model key, or a local model. Billed to you, under your agreement with the provider. Nothing routes through us.
  • A read-only database user. You create it, and the exact grants are published. It is refused if it tries to write.
  • Every query audited against the ticket that caused it — connector, parameters, rows returned, milliseconds.
  • An egress allowlist of three hosts. Nothing else is reachable outbound.
  • SSO on the console. One way in, through the identity provider you already run.
  • Telemetry is opt-in and content-free. Counts and timings. Never a ticket, never a note.

And the one that usually ends the conversation early: DNDD never writes to your ticket system. It posts to its own review queue, which only your team can see. Writing back is not a setting we left switched off — it is not implemented.
